Path 01 / Repair

Keep the asset you know.

Repair is often attractive when the motor is serviceable, compatible parts remain available, downtime needs to stay short and replacement lead times are uncertain.

Path 02 / Replace

Move to a newer platform.

Replacement becomes stronger when the motor is obsolete, inefficient, damaged beyond practical repair or no longer supported by available parts.

    Decision framework

    Do not choose on purchase price alone.

    EASA's repair-versus-replace guidance highlights availability, cost, root cause, maintenance history and repair quality as important decision inputs. This prototype turns those ideas into a faster visual decision path.

    01 / Condition

    How damaged is it?

    Mechanical reconditioning, bearing replacement or rewinding may be practical when the core asset remains structurally sound.

    02 / Availability

    Can a replacement arrive?

    Long lead times can make repair the more operationally useful option even when a replacement looks attractive on paper.

    03 / Age

    Is the motor obsolete?

    Older equipment becomes harder to justify when parts are discontinued, efficiency is poor or the application has changed.

    04 / Duty

    Is it specialized?

    Custom or hard-to-replace motors may deserve repair even at lower horsepower because like-for-like replacement can be difficult.

    05 / Downtime

    What does each day cost?

    The operational cost of waiting can outweigh the difference between repair and replacement purchase cost.

    06 / Future

    How long must it serve?

    Expected service life, efficiency targets and future process requirements should influence the final decision.

    Side-by-side

    Should you repair or replace your electric motor?

    Use this table as a screening tool. Final feasibility still depends on the exact motor, failure condition, parts availability and production requirements.

    FactorRepairReplace
    CostOften about 30–60% of the cost of a new motor, depending on damage and labor.Full new-motor cost plus possible shipping and installation impacts.
    Lead timeStandard turnaround may be around two weeks; rush work may be available.Depends on model and supply chain; some replacements can require extended lead times.
    Motor ageStronger when the motor is not obsolete and compatible parts remain available.Stronger when the existing motor is outdated or no longer well supported.
    Energy efficiencyGenerally preserves the original motor's efficiency class and operating characteristics.Newer models may offer improved efficiency classes and updated features.
    PartsFeasible when OEM or compatible repair parts can still be sourced.Preferred when critical parts are obsolete or unavailable.

    SustainabilityExtends the useful life of an existing asset and reduces disposal.May improve operating efficiency but creates a retired asset to manage.
    A practical rule

    Under 100 HP does not automatically mean replace.

    For many standard motors at 100 HP and below, replacement can be more economical than a full rewind. Exceptions include specialized or custom motors and cases where replacement lead times are too long.
